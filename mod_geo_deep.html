The Geometric Soul of Linear Algebra

Matrix = Geometric Transformation

Core Idea A matrix IS a function on space

Every \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\) defines a function \(f: \R^n \to \R^m\) by \(f(x) = Ax\). The columns of \(A\) tell you where the basis vectors land.

\[A = \begin{pmatrix} | & | \\ Ae_1 & Ae_2 \\ | & | \end{pmatrix}\]

Column \(j\) of \(A\) = the image of the \(j\)-th standard basis vector \(e_j\).

Think of it like this: a matrix is a recipe. It tells you: "send \(e_1\) here, send \(e_2\) there." Since the map is linear, everything else follows: any vector \(v = x_1 e_1 + x_2 e_2\) gets mapped to \(x_1 (Ae_1) + x_2 (Ae_2)\).

Gallery What common matrices look like

Identity \(I\): Does nothing. Every vector maps to itself.

Scaling \(\begin{pmatrix}k&0\\0&k\end{pmatrix}\): Uniform zoom by factor \(k\).

Stretch \(\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\0&b\end{pmatrix}\): Stretch \(x\)-axis by \(a\), \(y\)-axis by \(b\). If \(a \ne b\), circles become ellipses.

Rotation \(\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\): Rotate by \(\theta\) counterclockwise. Preserves lengths and angles.

Reflection \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\): Reflect across the \(x\)-axis.

Shear \(\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\): Slides points horizontally proportional to their \(y\). Turns rectangles into parallelograms.

Projection \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\): Collapses onto the \(x\)-axis. All vertical information is lost.

Determinant = Signed Volume Factor

Core Idea What \(\det A\) really means
\[\det A = \text{signed factor by which } A \text{ scales volumes}\]

|det A| = 2: areas double. |det A| = 0.5: areas halve. det A = 0: dimension collapses (flat).

Sign: positive = orientation preserved; negative = orientation flipped (like a reflection).

Take the unit square (vertices at origin, \(e_1\), \(e_2\), \(e_1+e_2\)). After applying \(A\), it becomes a parallelogram. Its area = \(|\det A|\). If the vertices go clockwise instead of counterclockwise, \(\det A < 0\).

Deep Point Why \(\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B\)

Applying \(A\) then \(B\): first scale volumes by \(\det A\), then by \(\det B\). Total scaling = product. This is why composition of transformations corresponds to multiplication of matrices and multiplication of determinants.

Example: \(\det\begin{pmatrix} 3&1\\0&2 \end{pmatrix} = 6\)

The unit square gets mapped to a parallelogram with vertices at \((0,0)\), \((3,0)\), \((1,2)\), \((4,2)\).

Area = base × height = 3 × 2 = 6 ✓. Positive sign: orientation preserved (counterclockwise stays counterclockwise).

Kernel & Image: What survives, what's lost

Core Idea Ker = what gets killed, Im = what gets reached

\(\Ker A\): all vectors that \(A\) maps to \(\vec{0}\). These are the "invisible" directions — the dimensions that get collapsed.

\(\Im A\): all vectors that can be reached as outputs. This is the "effective range" of the transformation.

Imagine projecting 3D space onto a plane. The kernel is the direction perpendicular to the plane (everything along this line gets crushed to zero). The image is the plane itself (every output lands here). Rank-nullity: 3 = 1 + 2.

Theorem Rank-Nullity (Geometric Version)
\[\underbrace{\dim \R^n}_{\text{original space}} = \underbrace{\dim \Ker A}_{\text{collapsed dimensions}} + \underbrace{\rank A}_{\text{surviving dimensions}}\]

Think of a tablecloth (2D) being crumpled onto a line (1D). One dimension survives (\(\rank = 1\)), one is lost (\(\dim \Ker = 1\)). Total = 2. The theorem says you can't create or destroy dimensions — you can only redistribute them between "surviving" and "killed".

Change of Basis: Same map, different coordinates

Core Idea \(A' = P^{-1}AP\)

The linear map \(f\) doesn't change — only your coordinate system changes. \(P\) converts from new basis to old basis.

\[\underbrace{P^{-1}}_{\text{new coords}} \underbrace{A}_{\text{apply map}} \underbrace{P}_{\text{old coords}} = A'\]

Imagine you're in Paris and someone gives you directions in miles. You convert miles → km, follow the directions, then convert the result km → miles. The physical path is the same; only the units changed. \(P\) is the unit converter.

Method Transition Matrix

If \(\mathcal{B} = (b_1, \ldots, b_n)\) is the old basis and \(\mathcal{B}' = (b'_1, \ldots, b'_n)\) is the new basis:

\(P_{\mathcal{B}' \to \mathcal{B}}\) has columns = coordinates of new basis vectors in the old basis.

Formula: \([v]_{\mathcal{B}} = P \cdot [v]_{\mathcal{B}'}\) and \([v]_{\mathcal{B}'} = P^{-1} \cdot [v]_{\mathcal{B}}\).

Worked Example: Rotation is diagonal in the right basis

\(R_{45°} = \begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt2}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\\\frac{\sqrt2}{2}&\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\end{pmatrix}\) in the standard basis.

Over \(\C\), eigenvalues are \(e^{\pm i\pi/4}\). In the eigenbasis, \(R_{45°}\) becomes \(\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\pi/4}&0\\0&e^{-i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix}\).

A rotation in 2D has no real eigenvectors (nothing stays on its line!). But in complex coordinates, the two "circular" modes \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) decouple perfectly. This is the deep reason Fourier analysis works: rotations become diagonal in the frequency basis.

Rank: The true dimension of information

Core Idea Rank = effective dimension of the output

\(\rank(A)\) = dimension of the column space = number of "independent directions" in the image.

Full rank (\(\rank = \min(m,n)\)): no dimension is wasted. The map is as "spread out" as possible.

Rank deficient: some dimensions collapse. Output lives in a lower-dimensional subspace.

A \(1000 \times 1000\) matrix with rank 3 is like a movie projector: it takes a 1000-dimensional input but the output lives on a 3D "screen". The other 997 dimensions are redundant. This is why low-rank approximation (SVD) is the foundation of data compression.

Systems \(Ax = b\): Geometry of solutions

Core Idea Three cases, geometrically

Case 1: \(b \in \Im A\) and \(\Ker A = \{0\}\) → Unique solution. The map is bijective, so there's exactly one preimage.

Case 2: \(b \in \Im A\) but \(\Ker A \ne \{0\}\) → Infinitely many solutions. One particular solution \(x_0\) plus any element of \(\Ker A\): the solution set is the affine subspace \(x_0 + \Ker A\).

Case 3: \(b \notin \Im A\) → No solution. The target \(b\) is "off the screen".

For a \(2 \times 2\) system with \(\Ker A = \) a line through origin: solutions form a line parallel to the kernel, shifted to pass through any particular solution. All solution lines for different \(b\) are parallel.

Theorem Rouché-Capelli (Kronecker-Capelli)
\[Ax = b \text{ has a solution} \iff \rank(A) = \rank(A|b)\]

The augmented matrix \((A|b)\) has the same rank as \(A\) iff \(b\) is in the column space of \(A\).

When solutions exist, the solution space has dimension \(n - \rank(A)\).

Worked Example: Geometric interpretation of a \(3 \times 3\) system

\[\begin{cases} x+y+z=1 \\ 2x+2y+2z=2 \\ x-y+z=0 \end{cases}\]

Row 2 = 2 × Row 1 (redundant). So rank = 2, but \(n = 3\). Solution space has dim = 1 (a line).

Geometrically: equations 1 and 2 define the same plane. Equation 3 defines a different plane. Their intersection = a line in \(\R^3\).

Parametrically: from row 1 and 3: \(z = 1-x-y\) and \(x+z = y \implies y = x + (1-x-y) \implies 2y = 1 \implies y = 1/2\). Then \(x + z = 1/2\), so \(x = t, z = 1/2 - t\). Solution: \((t, 1/2, 1/2-t)\), a line.

Inner Product: Length, angle, projection

Core Idea Dot product = "how much does \(u\) point in the direction of \(v\)?"
\[\langle u, v \rangle = \|u\| \cdot \|v\| \cos \theta\]

If \(\theta = 0\): vectors aligned, dot product maximal. If \(\theta = 90°\): orthogonal, dot product zero. If \(\theta = 180°\): opposed, dot product minimal (most negative).

Projection formula: The shadow of \(u\) onto \(v\) has length \(\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|v\|}\). This is the foundation of Fourier analysis (project a signal onto sinusoids) and least squares (project data onto a model).

Application Orthogonal Projection Matrix
\[P_v = \frac{vv^T}{v^Tv}\]

\(P_v u\) = projection of \(u\) onto the line spanned by \(v\). Note: \(P_v^2 = P_v\) (projecting twice = projecting once) and \(P_v^T = P_v\) (symmetric).

For projection onto a subspace spanned by columns of \(Q\) (orthonormal): \(P = QQ^T\).

Extension: SVD — The Ultimate Decomposition

Preview Singular Value Decomposition
\[A = U \Sigma V^T\]

Every matrix — any size, any rank — can be decomposed as: rotate (\(V^T\)) → scale (\(\Sigma\)) → rotate (\(U\)).

The \(\sigma_i\) (singular values) tell you how much stretching happens along each principal axis. Eigenvalues are only defined for square matrices; SVD works for everything.

A linear map sends the unit sphere to an ellipsoid. The singular values are the semi-axis lengths of this ellipsoid, and \(U, V\) encode the orientation. SVD = the ellipsoid decomposition. This is why it's the backbone of PCA, recommender systems, image compression, and pseudo-inverses.
