特征值与对角化

Valeurs propres, vecteurs propres et diagonalisation

基本定义

Definition Eigenvalue & Eigenvector
\[A v = \lambda v, \quad v \ne \vec{0}\]

A scalar \(\lambda \in \K\) is an eigenvalue (valeur propre) of \(A \in M_n(\K)\) if \(\exists v \ne 0\) such that \(Av = \lambda v\). The non-zero vector \(v\) is called an eigenvector (vecteur propre).

An eigenvector is a direction that the linear map does not rotate — it only stretches or compresses it by factor \(\lambda\). If \(\lambda > 0\), same direction. If \(\lambda < 0\), reversed. If \(\lambda = 1\), fixed. If \(\lambda = 0\), collapsed to origin (in the kernel).

Definition Eigenspace
\[E_\lambda = \Ker(A - \lambda I) = \{v \in \K^n : Av = \lambda v\}\]

\(E_\lambda\) is a subspace. Its dimension \(\dim E_\lambda\) is called the geometric multiplicity (multiplicité géométrique).

The algebraic multiplicity (multiplicité algébrique) \(m_\lambda\) is the multiplicity of \(\lambda\) as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

Key inequality: \(1 \le \dim E_\lambda \le m_\lambda\)

Definition Spectrum & Characteristic Polynomial
\[\chi_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)\]

The spectrum \(\Sp(A)\) is the set of all eigenvalues: the roots of \(\chi_A\).

For \(A \in M_n(\R)\), \(\chi_A\) is a degree-\(n\) polynomial in \(\lambda\). It has at most \(n\) real roots.

计算方法

Method Finding Eigenvalues (Step by Step)

Step 1. Compute \(\chi_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)\).

Step 2. Solve \(\chi_A(\lambda) = 0\) to find eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k\).

Step 3. For each \(\lambda_i\), solve \((A - \lambda_i I)x = 0\) to find the eigenspace \(E_{\lambda_i}\).

Worked Example: \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\)

Step 1. \(\chi_A(\lambda) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 4-\lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3-\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10\)

Step 2. \(\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda-2)(\lambda-5) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 2,\; \lambda_2 = 5\)

Step 3a. \(E_2: (A-2I)x=0 \implies \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}x=0 \implies x_1 = -\frac{1}{2}x_2 \implies E_2 = \Vect\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\)

Step 3b. \(E_5: (A-5I)x=0 \implies \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}x=0 \implies x_1 = x_2 \implies E_5 = \Vect\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\)

The vector \((-1,2)^T\) gets scaled by 2 (stretched), and \((1,1)^T\) gets scaled by 5. Any other vector is a mix of both and gets "sheared" — but in the eigenbasis, the map is just two independent scalings.

Worked Example: \(3 \times 3\) matrix with repeated eigenvalue

Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&3 \end{pmatrix}\)

\(\chi_A(\lambda) = (2-\lambda)^2(3-\lambda)\), so \(\lambda_1 = 2\) (algebraic mult. 2) and \(\lambda_2 = 3\) (mult. 1).

\(E_2: (A-2I)x = 0 \implies \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}x = 0 \implies x_2 = x_3 = 0,\; x_1\) free. So \(E_2 = \Vect(e_1)\), dim = 1.

Here geometric mult (1) < algebraic mult (2): \(A\) is NOT diagonalizable.

\(E_3 = \Vect(e_3)\), dim = 1. Total eigenvectors span only a 2D subspace of \(\R^3\): not enough for a basis.

The "missing" eigenvector at \(\lambda=2\) means there's a shearing in the \(e_1\)-\(e_2\) plane that can't be undone by a change of basis. This is precisely the Jordan block \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\).

速算技巧

Shortcut Trace & Determinant
\[\tr(A) = \sum \lambda_i, \qquad \det(A) = \prod \lambda_i\]

For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, this gives you both eigenvalues immediately: they're the roots of \(\lambda^2 - \tr(A)\lambda + \det(A) = 0\).

Shortcut Triangular Matrix

If \(A\) is upper or lower triangular, the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries. No computation needed.

Shortcut Rank-1 Updates

If \(A = I + uv^T\) (rank-1 perturbation of identity), then eigenvalues are \(1 + v^Tu\) (once) and \(1\) (with multiplicity \(n-1\)).

Trap Common Mistakes

① \(\det(A - \lambda I) \ne \det(A) - \lambda\). You must expand properly.

② Eigenvectors are non-zero by definition. \(\vec{0}\) is never an eigenvector.

③ \(\lambda = 0\) is a perfectly valid eigenvalue. It means \(\Ker A \ne \{0\}\) (A is singular).

④ Eigenspaces of distinct eigenvalues always intersect trivially: \(E_{\lambda_1} \cap E_{\lambda_2} = \{0\}\).

对角化

Theorem Diagonalization Criterion
\[A \text{ is diagonalizable} \iff \forall \lambda_i \in \Sp(A): \dim E_{\lambda_i} = m_{\lambda_i}\]

Equivalently: \(A\) is diagonalizable iff there exists a basis of \(\K^n\) consisting entirely of eigenvectors of \(A\).

Sufficient condition: If \(A \in M_n\) has \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, then \(A\) is diagonalizable.

Method Diagonalizing \(A = PDP^{-1}\)

Step 1. Find all eigenvalues and eigenspaces.

Step 2. Check: total number of independent eigenvectors = \(n\)?

Step 3. Form \(P\) = matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors.

Step 4. \(D = \diag(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)\), in the same column order as \(P\).

Verification: \(AP = PD\) (check column by column: \(Av_i = \lambda_i v_i\)).

\(P\) is a change of basis to the eigenbasis. In this new coordinate system, the linear map is just independent scalings along each axis. \(P^{-1}\) converts to eigenbasis, \(D\) scales, \(P\) converts back.

Worked Example: Full diagonalization of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1&2\\2&1 \end{pmatrix}\)

\(\chi_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = (\lambda-3)(\lambda+1)\). Eigenvalues: \(\lambda_1=3, \lambda_2=-1\).

\(E_3: \begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}x=0 \implies v_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\)

\(E_{-1}: \begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}x=0 \implies v_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\)

\(P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad D = \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\)

Application: \(A^{100} = PD^{100}P^{-1} = P\begin{pmatrix}3^{100}&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}P^{-1}\)

\(A\) is a symmetric matrix. Its eigenvectors \((1,1)^T\) and \((1,-1)^T\) are orthogonal (this always happens for symmetric matrices). The map stretches by 3 along the diagonal \(y=x\) and reflects (\(\times(-1)\)) along \(y=-x\).

Worked Example: Fibonacci via diagonalization

The recurrence \(F_{n+2} = F_{n+1} + F_n\) can be written as \(\begin{pmatrix}F_{n+1}\\F_n\end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}}_{M} \begin{pmatrix}F_n\\F_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\).

\(\chi_M(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \lambda - 1\), roots: \(\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\) and \(\psi = \frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}\).

After diagonalization: \(M^n = P\begin{pmatrix}\varphi^n&0\\0&\psi^n\end{pmatrix}P^{-1}\), giving Binet's formula:

\[F_n = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt{5}}\]

The golden ratio \(\varphi\) is the dominant eigenvalue. As \(n \to \infty\), the system aligns with the eigenvector of \(\varphi\), so \(F_{n+1}/F_n \to \varphi\). The minor eigenvalue \(|\psi| < 1\) decays exponentially — it's the "transient" component.

Applications & Deep Results

Application Powers of Matrices

If \(A = PDP^{-1}\), then \(A^k = PD^kP^{-1}\). This reduces matrix exponentiation to scalar exponentiation.

For \(e^{tA}\) (matrix exponential, used in ODE \(\dot{x} = Ax\)):

\[e^{tA} = P \begin{pmatrix} e^{t\lambda_1} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & e^{t\lambda_n} \end{pmatrix} P^{-1}\]

Each eigendirection evolves independently: if \(\lambda_i > 0\), that direction explodes; if \(\lambda_i < 0\), it decays; if \(\lambda_i = 0\), it's stationary. This is the foundation of stability analysis in dynamical systems and neural ODEs.

Application Symmetric Matrices (Spectral Theorem)
\[A = A^T \implies A = Q\Lambda Q^T, \quad Q \text{ orthogonal } (Q^{-1} = Q^T)\]

Symmetric matrices are always diagonalizable, with real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors.

A symmetric matrix represents a map that only stretches/compresses along orthogonal axes — no rotation, no shearing. The eigenvectors are the "principal axes". This is why PCA works: the covariance matrix is symmetric, and its eigenvectors are the directions of maximum variance.

Extension Cayley-Hamilton Theorem
\[\chi_A(A) = 0\]

Every matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation. For \(2 \times 2\): \(A^2 - \tr(A) \cdot A + \det(A) \cdot I = 0\).

Application: Express \(A^{-1}\) without computing it directly: \(A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}(\tr(A) \cdot I - A)\) (for \(2 \times 2\)).

Extension Gershgorin Circle Theorem

Every eigenvalue of \(A\) lies in at least one Gershgorin disc:

\[D_i = \left\{\lambda \in \C : |\lambda - a_{ii}| \le \sum_{j \ne i} |a_{ij}|\right\}\]

Center = diagonal entry, radius = sum of absolute off-diagonal entries in that row. Useful for quick eigenvalue bounds without computing \(\chi_A\).

Extension AI Connection: Eigenvalues Everywhere

PCA: Eigenvectors of covariance matrix = principal components. Eigenvalues = explained variance.

PageRank: Google's algorithm finds the dominant eigenvector of the web's adjacency matrix.

Graph Neural Networks: Spectral convolutions use eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian \(L = D - A\).

Stability of training: The Hessian's eigenvalues determine if you're at a minimum (all \(\lambda > 0\)), maximum (all \(\lambda < 0\)), or saddle point (mixed signs).

Exercises

⭐ Exercise 1: Eigenvalues of a projection

Q: Let \(P\) be a projection matrix (\(P^2 = P\)). Show that the only possible eigenvalues are 0 and 1.

Solution: If \(Pv = \lambda v\) with \(v \ne 0\), then \(P^2 v = P(\lambda v) = \lambda Pv = \lambda^2 v\). But \(P^2 = P\), so \(P^2 v = Pv = \lambda v\). Thus \(\lambda^2 = \lambda \implies \lambda(\lambda - 1) = 0\).

A projection "flattens" space onto a subspace. Points already on the subspace stay fixed (\(\lambda=1\)), points orthogonal to it collapse to zero (\(\lambda=0\)). No other behavior is possible.

⭐ Exercise 2: Nilpotent matrix

Q: If \(A^k = 0\) for some \(k \ge 1\), show that every eigenvalue of \(A\) is 0.

Solution: If \(Av = \lambda v\), then \(A^k v = \lambda^k v\). But \(A^k = 0\), so \(\lambda^k v = 0\). Since \(v \ne 0\), \(\lambda^k = 0 \implies \lambda = 0\).

Converse is false: Not every matrix with all eigenvalues 0 is nilpotent (it is, but this requires Jordan theory to prove).

⭐⭐ Exercise 3: \(AB\) and \(BA\) have the same eigenvalues

Q: Let \(A \in M_{m \times n}\), \(B \in M_{n \times m}\). Show that \(AB\) and \(BA\) have the same non-zero eigenvalues.

Solution: Let \(\lambda \ne 0\) with \(ABv = \lambda v\). Set \(w = Bv\). Then \(BAw = BA(Bv) = B(ABv) = B(\lambda v) = \lambda Bv = \lambda w\). Is \(w \ne 0\)? Yes: if \(w = Bv = 0\), then \(ABv = A \cdot 0 = 0 = \lambda v\), so \(\lambda = 0\), contradiction.

Application: For \(A \in M_{1000 \times 3}\) and \(B \in M_{3 \times 1000}\), \(AB\) is \(1000 \times 1000\) but \(BA\) is only \(3 \times 3\). Compute eigenvalues of the small matrix!

⭐⭐ Exercise 4: Diagonalize \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0 \end{pmatrix}\) (permutation matrix)

\(A\) is the cyclic permutation \((1\;2\;3)\). Since \(A^3 = I\), eigenvalues satisfy \(\lambda^3 = 1\).

Roots: \(\lambda = 1, \omega, \omega^2\) where \(\omega = e^{2i\pi/3} = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i\).

Over \(\C\): three distinct eigenvalues → diagonalizable. Over \(\R\): only \(\lambda = 1\) is real → NOT diagonalizable over \(\R\).

This matrix rotates 3D space around the axis \((1,1,1)\) by \(120°\). The only real "fixed direction" is \((1,1,1)\) itself (\(\lambda=1\)). The complex eigenvalues encode the rotation angle.

⭐⭐⭐ Exercise 5: Prove the Spectral Theorem for \(2 \times 2\)

Q: Show that any real symmetric \(2 \times 2\) matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors.

Solution: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & d \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(\chi_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad - b^2)\).

Discriminant: \(\Delta = (a+d)^2 - 4(ad - b^2) = (a-d)^2 + 4b^2 \ge 0\). So roots are always real.

For orthogonality: if \(\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2\) with \(Av_1 = \lambda_1 v_1, Av_2 = \lambda_2 v_2\):

\[\lambda_1 \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = \langle Av_1, v_2 \rangle = \langle v_1, A^Tv_2 \rangle = \langle v_1, Av_2 \rangle = \lambda_2 \langle v_1, v_2 \rangle\]

\((\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = 0\). Since \(\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2\), we get \(\langle v_1, v_2 \rangle = 0\). ∎
